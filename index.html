Nite Thrush: The Ghost of Tuckahoe Creek

One of Western Richmond's most often-shared ghost stories has more than one victim.

by Jimmy Marks

Tuckahoe Creek is a well-known feature of Richmond’s Far West End, drawing a rough dividing line between Henrico County and Goochland County to the West. Birders of all ages flock to the creek to see a wide variety of birds that call the creek their home. But even the most skilled birder would likely not recognize the song of a Nite Thrush.

The Nite Thrush (named "n-i-t-e" by its discoverer and spelled that way ever since) was a bird common to the area at the start of the 20th century and for many years before. But in the span of fifty years, the bird would go from abundant populations to total extinction. While human population sprawl and ecological issues can be blamed in part, the greatest harm done to the species was all the work of one man.

The story of the extinction of the Nite Thrush coincides with one of the most shocking murder trials in the history of early-20th-century Richmond…and with the emergence of one of the area’s most well-told ghost stories – the Ghost of Tuckahoe Creek.

Colonel Hiram Dredge was a military man with a head for business and a passion for wildlife. After arriving home from World War I, Dredge wanted to stake out a quiet life for himself and his family: his loving wife Melvina and his beautiful young daughter, Lucy. Dredge parlayed his combat pay and his private holdings into several successful businesses and, with the windfall, built a mansion in the West End of Richmond, Virginia, and named it “HolyOak.”

The Dredge family enjoyed life in the country, far from the hustle-and-bustle of 1920s Richmond. They used their wealth to retain a number of servants and workers to help run the estate and spent their days in leisure on the banks of Tuckahoe Creek. Colonel Dredge enjoyed spending his hours birding, writing down daily reports of the species he spied in his journals and even making some well-done watercolor drawings of especially attractive species. It was there on the banks of the creek that the Colonel first spied a species he’d never heard of before the family’s move to the countryside: a Nite Thrush.

The Nite Thrush was an especially rare and beautiful bird, one that could only be see in the very early late hours of dawn or the very early hours of dusk. It had a mournful call and beautiful plumage. The Colonel only happened to see one by very seldom chance one evening when walking after a meal. He didn’t have time to make a drawing of it but the sight of it held his interest for days after his encounter.

The Colonel, having moved away from many of his friends in Richmond, was hoping to make a few new friends out west and decided he would form a birdwatching group. He put an advertisement in the Richmond Times Dispatch, looking for “Any man willing to join a society dedicated to birding and to the enjoyment and appreciation of the Lord’s splendid design.” Several passionate birdwatchers in the West End replied to the Colonel’s advertisement and, in the summer of 1926, joined the Colonel at HolyOak for the first ever meeting of the Greater Richmond Birdwatching Society.

No one - not the Colonel, his family, or his newfound friends - could have predicted what would happen next. At that simple gathering, the seeds were sown for one of the most harrowing murder cases in Richmond’s history.

The Greater Richmond Birdwatching Society met every two weeks for the first few months after their establishment. The society was small, made up mostly of well-to-do men who had leisure time and the means to travel about, looking for new species of bird. Many of them had seen a Nite Thrush only in passing, as the Colonel had, but were eager to see one in better detail.

Equally eager to see a Nite Thrush was young Lucy, the Colonel’s daughter. At just 17 years of age, Lucy was already an accomplished painter and, like her father, a budding birder and nature-lover who enjoyed accompanying her father when he saw fit to take her along on his hikes through the countryside. Melvina, Lucy’s mother, disapproved of Lucy being too interested; she thought young women should make every effort to secure a husband and to marry well. Lucy wasn’t keen on the idea of marriage; nor was her father, the Colonel. The Colonel once wrote in his journals:

It is the secret hope of my heart that my Lucy becomes a spinster and consents to keeping her dear old father company for the rest of her days. To give her away to another would be too taxing on my spirit.

Lucy would sometimes attend meetings of the Greater Richmond Birdwatching Society as its “Secretary,” keeping notes on the men’s findings and meeting minutes that the men could refer to in later gatherings. The men of the society enjoyed Lucy’s company and found her witty, charming, and a capable birder on her own with a terrific memory for detail. Lucy’s charms weren’t lost on the younger members of the group, both single and in the market for a wife - especially one with a father as well-off as the Colonel was.

The elder of the two young men, Francis Mint, was already a member of the Richmond elite, born to a wealthy and respected family in town. Mint was an accountant by trade and used his own personal holdings to fund his trips afield to see birds in different habitats and to visit museums and explore their catalogs. Mint found Lucy beguiling and set about to try and win her interest. He had, in his travels, found the feathers of a Nite Thrush and presented them to Lucy herself as a gift. Lucy was intrigued, and the brilliant plumage made her want to see a Nite Thrush that much more.

The other competitor for Lucy’s affection was the youngest society member, Caldwell Levin. Levin had no family fortune - his own success had come as a surveyor and prospector working in the West End and beyond into neighboring Goochland. What little Levin had, he’d earned on his own, and his knowledge of the West End and of Tuckahoe Creek was extensive. Levin presented the group with not only an array of feathers, but an intact nest and some hatched Nite Thrush eggs as well.

While neither man was unkind to the other, both worked extensively on gaining Lucy’s interest. Lucy, for her part, found both men to be well-mannered, kind and interesting. Though the race for her affection seemed to be neck-and-neck for the longest time, Levin made a move that put him ahead of Mint: he began to leave notes and letters for Lucy in the foyer and the study of HolyOak. He would give her a subtle hint about where she might find a new letter and she would set about finding it, like an Easter egg hunt. When she found them, she’d replace the letter he’d written with a new one of her own on the next visit. Levin would read Lucy’s correspondence and the game would begin again. Over several visits, Levin wrote Lucy letters that became more and more romantic in nature. Lucy’s replies, whatever they might have been, were lost to time, but Levin’s letters were kept by Lucy underneath a drawer in her nightstand.

The last letter Lucy received from Levin was a proposal; not of marriage, but of a secret journey the two might take together. Levin had knowledge of a populated Nite Thrush nesting ground and was eager to take Lucy to see live Nite Thrushes for herself. Melvina, Lucy’s mother, rarely let Lucy out of the house after dusk, but Levin promised that if Lucy would accompany him, he would make her trip worthwhile.

So it was that, on a cool October night in 1926, Lucy Dredge retired to her room early and quietly made her way outside in the twilight to meet Caldwell Levin in the hopes that her would-be suitor would be able to produce a real-live Nite Thrush.

By morning, both she and Levin would be gone.

Melvina Dredge awoke in the pre-dawn hours on the morning of October 27, 1926, and sought to wake her daughter to begin preparations for the day’s events. When Melvina entered Lucy’s room, she found nothing but a heap of blankets under Lucy’s comforter. Curious, Melvina toured the halls of HolyOak, calling softly for her daughter. When she’d made her way through the major rooms of the house and found nothing, Melvina’s calls grew louder and louder. The Colonel and several members of the household staff heard Melvina’s cries and the hunt for Lucy began.

The groundskeepers at HolyOak set about looking around the yard, the stable, the garage – all the places they could think to look on the grounds. Eventually, they leapt the split-rail fence and headed toward the creek. One of them, an older man named George, began to ring a cow bell he’d brought with him, hoping to summon the others to the creekside.

There, among the reeds, lay poor Lucy Dredge.

In the ensuing chaos, a local sheriff was summoned. He and what few men he had began to examine the area, looking for any clues as to what might have happened. Eventually, a more seasoned investigator and a doctor from the city of Richmond made their way to the scene and determined what had caused Lucy’s demise.

The poor child was strangled, quickly and purposefully. There are no signs of violent struggle or of heinous action beyond her strangulation – the perpetrator made quick and decisive work of it and threw her into the creek to cover his tracks. The only other bit we have to go on is a cracked spyglass, like the sort one might use in hunting or stargazing. Its origins are at this moment unclear.

The days that followed were bitter and bleak for the Dredge family. The investigator combed the house and found Levin’s letters under the drawer in Lucy’s room. A search for Levin turned up nothing. His modest cabin at the very edge of the creek and Goochland County had been ransacked and there was no sign of Levin’s whereabouts. Further investigation revealed that “Caldwell Levin” was a pseudonym; Levin’s real name was Burnham Gooch, a member of the sprawling Gooch family of Goochland County. Burnham Gooch’s family was poor, too poor to afford a proper education. So, at a very early age, young Burnham went into an apprenticeship with a surveyor and land manager who taught him the tricks of the trade. Eager to leave behind his life of poverty and toil, Burnham Gooch renamed himself Caldwell Levin and began to amass a reasonable personal wealth.

This bit of deceit was all the investigators - and the Dredge family - needed to decide that Caldwell Levin was the man who had done the unspeakable crime they now had to face. A posse was formed and several men rode the countryside, seeking any trace of Levin they could find. For weeks, rumors and reports would put him here and there around Richmond, but there was never any clear indication of where Levin might be. After a month and a half of vigorous searching, the investigation ran cold, and the search for Lucy’s killer lost steam. The only other thread detectives had to pull was the origin of the spyglass. A glassblower in the Fan District of Richmond had filled an order for a custom pair of spyglasses with a Nite Thrush etched on their side. In his records, it seemed that Caldwell Levin had been the buyer and had paid in full and received the spyglasses. Though detectives searched, they hadn’t recovered the other spyglass, but assumed Levin had it on his person and it was still in his possession where he had fled.

The Dredge family buried Lucy in a simple grave on the grounds of HolyOak. Melvina’s health began to fail not long after that and she had to be kept by a live-in nurse. The Colonel, for his part, sought to keep life at HolyOak as pleasant as he could, trying in vain to avoid the sorrow in his heart. The Greater Richmond Birdwatching Society continued to meet here and there, but attendance waned over time and many of the members - the Colonel included - began to attend meetings with other organizations, closer to the city.

It would seem that the truth of what happened to Lucy would be lost to time. But nearly one year later, when all the news of the Lucy Dredge case had run dry, a man walked in to the Sheriff’s office with a simple plea:

“I killed Lucy Dredge. Please arrest me.”

The police in Richmond City who’d taken up the broader investigation of Lucy’s killing were summoned by telephone to the offices of the Tuckahoe Sheriff. There, they sat across a table from Francis Mint, who looked, according to one of the detectives in attendance, “as close to dead as I’ve seen any living man.” Ashen, shaky, with now-graying hair and vacant eyes, Mint stared at the table in front of him and told the story to the lawmen surrounding him.

According to Mint himself, he was wise to Levin and Lucy’s notes. When Levin would hide one, Mint would take note, read it, and put it back. When a new one appeared from Lucy, Mint would do the same. Aware of what the two were planning, Mint formed a plan of his own: when Lucy snuck away to meet Levin and see the Nite Thrush nesting ground, Mint would interrupt them, shame Levin for his lack of honor, and escort Lucy home, where he would then request from a grateful Colonel Dredge Lucy’s hand in marriage.

Mint said that his plan mostly went according to his design, but with one twist: Levin grew agitated when confronted and began to shout at Mint, telling Mint the two were in love and their doings were none of his business. The two began to quarrel. Mint, the larger of the two men, got the upper hand. He grabbed the spyglass from Lucy’s hands and smashed Levin in the head with it. As best Mint could tell, the blow killed Levin almost instantly.

Lucy screamed. She was terrified of Mint and of what he might do. Fearing that she would testify against him in a court of law or, worse yet, to her father who might not wait for justice to be done, Mint grabbed Lucy and strangled her. In the tumult, Mint quickly formed a plan; he would leave the broken spyglass with Lucy’s body and drop both into the creek. He would then take Levin’s body and dispose of it elsewhere, making it seem that Levin had fled. Mint went to Levin’s home and tore the place to pieces, hoping to convince investigators that Levin had gathered his meager possessions and left in a hurry. When pressed about the location of Levin’s body, Mint grew quiet. He refused to ever reveal the location of Levin’s remains. The one piece of evidence he could produce sat in the pocket of his coat; the twin spyglass, given to Lucy as a would-be proposal gift, a dowry of a kind. Mint admitted that the beauty of the item delighted him and he thought he might one day be able to sell it without anyone making the connection to the crime.

Detectives were flabbergasted; at last, one of them asked why Mint would come back and confess to a crime he’d already gotten away with, one that admitting to might cost Mint his own life on the gallows.

Mint told the investigators that he was out one evening, looking for migrating flocks of birds, when he saw a faint circle of light across the bank of Tuckahoe Creek.

Mint pulled his own spyglass out of his coat and stared at the glowing orb; he saw a body in the faint light of dusk, holding another spyglass up to its eye. The viewer did not budge, nor did they remove the spyglass from their own eye; they simply stared at Mint and walked closer and closer to his side of the creek bank.

Mint testified that he could hear the songs of what he described as “thousands, legions of Nite Thrushes, singing so loudly I could not hear myself think.”

The encounter shook Mint to his core. He gathered the spyglass and ran home. A strong drink and a sit by the fire soothed him ever so slightly, but as he rose to close the curtains to his room, the same faint glow of a spyglass lens stared at him from somewhere far off. He ventured another look out at the source of the light and, once again, saw the faint outline of a body holding the spyglass to its eye.

Whatever vision was stalking Mint convinced him that the truth would eventually reveal itself. Shaken by what he’d seen and heard, he turned himself in the very next day.

The confession Mint offered led to his immediate arrest. His trial, quick and fairly uncomplicated, happened a few weeks later. Mint plead guilty and refused to negotiate for lesser sentencing. When asked for comment during the sentencing hearing, Colonel Hiram Dredge waved away any talk of the death penalty.

“My Lucy is gone. No act of sacrifice can bring her back. Putting Francis Mint’s neck in a noose will only serve to end his suffering; nothing shall ever end mine.”

Mint, for his part, expressed remorse on the stand and in private conversations. He was sentenced to life in prison for the murder of Lucy, but could not be properly convicted for the death of Caldwell Levin - namely because no body was ever produced or discovered. Still, “criminal mischief and wrongdoing” was suitable enough to add another forty years to an already-lifelong sentence. Mint died decades later in prison, rarely speaking to another soul, but could often be heard waking in the night, terrified. It cannot be determined, but many speculate Mint continued to look out of windows and see the glow of the spyglass in the dark, and could hear the songs of the Nite Thrushes long into his old age.

If Mint heard these songs, however, he heard them only in his head. After Mint’s trial and Melvina’s eventual death, Colonel Hiram Dredge could no longer stand to hear or see any trace of Nite Thrushes in the natural world. The one-time founder of the Greater Richmond Birdwatching Society, who once expressed his affection for “the Lord’s splendid design,” was now on a mission: he hoped to eradicate the Nite Thrush in the vicinity of HolyOak.

A bounty was offered to hunters, fishermen, and outdoorsmen of all kinds: five dollars would be given if anyone could bring the body of a Nite Thrush to the Colonel himself. The word spread and, in the difficult years of the Great Depression, the Colonel spent considerable sums of money paying men of all ages for their quarry. One man reportedly brought the Colonel ten Nite Thrushes in a single afternoon, walking away with fifty dollars in cash.

Biologists and ecologists point to the Colonel’s bounty as one of the most destructive acts in the history of wildlife up to that point. The Colonel’s efforts to deplete the population were capped off years after his death, when DDT negatively impacted avian populations across the nation, and the Nite Thrush was no exception. What few Nite Thrushes still existed were afflicted by weakened egg shells and the species as a whole was considered functionally extinct in 1974. The last reported sighting of a Nite Thrush was in 1971, and even that sighting was not confirmed.

There are many versions of the tale of the Ghost of Tuckahoe Creek. Most depict the ghost as a young woman, draped in white lace, wandering the shores and in the marshes of Tuckahoe Creek, looking for her lost love. A small contingent of storytellers insists that the ghost is not young Lucy, but rather Caldwell Levin (aka Burnham Gooch), his body never recovered, searching helplessly for the girl he loved. Others still believe that the ghost is that of Colonel Hiram Dredge, haunted by not only what happened to his family, but what disasters he wrought on the wildlife he once loved so devotedly.

But the stories, no matter their subject, all seem to agree on a few key details. To see the ghost, one must venture to Tuckahoe Creek when the sky is dark. Wait long enough and you can see the reflection of the spyglass, catching what faint light it can, glaring out over the water and the marsh grasses. And if you listen closely, you can hear the faint, long-forgotten mating call of the Nite Thrush.
